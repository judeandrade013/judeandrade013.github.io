A resident comes with a task

A resident looking for a permit shouldn’t need to know which department handles it before they can begin. But municipal websites often reflect how government is organized internally rather than how residents experience a need. In Spring Valley, forms, payments, department pages, and service instructions lived in different places. The challenge wasn’t only visual inconsistency. A resident could find a document and still have no clear idea how to finish the task.

I followed the path beyond the link

I reviewed the website’s navigation, organized a public Forms Index, and worked on homepage hierarchy and access to common services. Facility requests became a useful test of the whole system, because the process had no online path at all. A resident needed to select a facility, choose a date, explain the event, and wait for staff approval. Behind the scenes, someone had to check availability, insurance, fees, and scheduling rules.

What I had to give up

My ideal experience was simple: pick the place, request the time, understand exactly what happens next. EvoGov’s native tools imposed real limits. The portal required login, permissions were too broad for some intended viewers, not every paper-form rule could be enforced automatically, and seasonal sunset cutoffs weren’t supported as requested. I worked within the controls we could validate instead of inventing an interface staff couldn’t operate.

The observable result

The Forms Index went live. Seven facility and stage resources were configured for public requests. Two controlled test records exercised confirmation and cancellation, and the public calendar reflected those status changes. That is a usable foundation, not a finished payment-and-permit service. The October 2026 flow map describes the next release, scheduled for early November 2026, with one reference number and approval before payment; it is not the operating system yet.

What stayed with me

The visible page was only half the problem. A simpler resident experience holds up only when staff can receive the work, interpret its status, and carry it to completion.

Protecting passengers was the stated goal. But punishing only the person behind the wheel could miss the dispatcher or fleet setting the terms, and burden legitimate small operators.

The story in four frames

30-second read
01The problem

A driver was not the whole business

Spring Valley depends on taxis, but unlicensed activity, passenger safety, and vehicle saturation made reform urgent. Conversations with smaller operators raised the real question: when a driver works through a dispatch or fleet arrangement, who controls access to the trip?

Judgment callLook upstream from the visible violation to whoever sets the terms.

02The investigation

The loophole lived in the definitions

Existing Chapter 239 categories struggled to describe dispatch through intermediaries, shared payments, and networks that don’t own vehicles. A crackdown built on vehicle counts and flat fees would also fall hardest on compliant owner-operators.

Judgment callClassify by actual behavior and control, not by branding or contract labels.

03The turn

Make accountability follow the trip

The drafts link operator, vehicle, and driver through rosters, trip records, and audits, with tiered fees and differentiated oversight for larger networks. Stronger oversight pulled against privacy, due process, and compliance cost.

Judgment callBuild the enforcement information first, then escalate penalties. Flag privacy and due-process questions instead of hiding them.

04The result and its limits

A more traceable framework took shape

The revised drafts make responsibility explicit for networks and fleets and move toward appearance-neutral affiliation standards. They are working drafts, not certified law, so no safety or congestion effects are claimed.

Judgment callKeep a drafted mechanism, an adopted law, and measured public impact separate.

What I owned

I reviewed the existing code and drafted revisions for fleet and transportation-network definitions, affiliation, trip records, audits, and licensing. I worked through the difference between a driver’s legal status and the operational control a larger network can exercise, and tested the drafts against smaller operators’ circumstances.

The reframe

Classify a business by what it actually does, not the label it uses or how its vehicles look.

What this doesn’t show

The reviewed documents are working and final-labelled drafts, not a certified ordinance. I don’t claim fewer accidents, fewer illegal taxis, or reduced congestion without implementation evidence.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Vehicle cap versus network accountability
Explored: capacity controls, but didn’t treat a cap as a substitute for identifying operators.
Driver penalty versus operator responsibility
Proposed: requirements that reach the entities controlling dispatch and vehicle access.
Uniform fees versus tiered fees
Proposed: fees that reflect operational scale while protecting smaller compliant businesses.
Visual branding versus evidence of control
Drafted: affiliation determined by conduct and coordination; some versions explicitly avoid appearance-based presumptions.

The crowded street was not the whole problem

Spring Valley had a transportation problem: safety concerns, traffic saturation, and disputes about unlicensed activity. But taxis were essential for residents and a livelihood for drivers, including operators who had already spent money following the rules. The obvious solution was to reduce the number of cabs and increase enforcement. As I dug into the system, I realized that might miss the structure underneath the problem.

The person driving was not always the person controlling

I reviewed Chapter 239 and spoke with smaller transportation businesses. I heard about drivers gaining access to customers through larger operators and informal dispatch arrangements. A driver could be the visible person taking the passenger while another entity arranged trips, collected fees, or set operating conditions. I began asking whether the regulations would recognize that relationship and where responsibility would land when things went wrong.

The definition became a design decision

The earlier law already regulated taxis, vehicles for hire, and licensing. What it didn’t consistently account for was the variety of ways trips could be coordinated without a conventional employer or vehicle owner. I worked on language that classified businesses by dispatching, routing, fee collection, shared systems, and actual control, not simply the label on an agreement. The proposed chain linked operator, vehicle, and driver and added rosters and trip records that could support investigation.

Fairness changed the draft

The more rules we proposed, the more the distribution of burden mattered. I didn’t want to penalize compliant owner-operators for the practices of larger coordinated networks. We considered tiered fees, fleet oversight, and checks against splitting a large operation into smaller paper entities to avoid regulation. Each measure has its own tradeoffs: record privacy, compliance cost, legal review, and the risk of overly punitive enforcement.

Where I draw the outcome line

The rewrite produced detailed proposals for operator-level accountability, explicit affiliation rules, and auditability. It is a drafted framework, not a measured result. I’d want comparative evidence before attributing lower congestion or fewer incidents to it.

What stayed with me

If regulation only sees what is visible at the curb, it can miss the system organizing the ride. A fairer rule has to identify the party capable of changing the behavior, not merely the easiest person to cite.

The problem was not new

Every time Pascack Brook flooded around Memorial Park, Spring Valley faced the consequences of a long-running problem. The park is one of the Village’s central recreational spaces, and nearby properties and infrastructure were also at risk. The Village had financial constraints and needed a viable funding path. I thought the hardest part might be discovering the engineering answer. I was wrong about where the real difficulty sat.

Following the paper trail

I looked for previous flood studies and found that the County and other agencies had already commissioned relevant work. Some hazard-mitigation sections were redacted or difficult to access. I submitted County FOIL requests for engineering records, consultant work, and the basis and status of a possible historical Village contribution. I wanted to know what had already been studied, who controlled the affected corridor, and what commitments were documented before anybody spent money or assigned blame.

Why the politics mattered

The Village was seeking County cooperation while also trying to untangle old financial obligations from earlier administrations. The work touched County-controlled stream areas and rail infrastructure. I couldn’t approach the problem as though the Village owned everything, and I couldn’t afford to damage working relationships by making claims the documents didn’t yet establish. The difference between acknowledging responsibility and granting a practical approval became essential.

The first turning point: study eligibility

The broad engineering solution wasn’t automatically suitable for the Resilient Watersheds Grant. DEC flagged gaps in the site-specific study’s future-flow analysis, and some interventions were outside the grant’s eligible scope. I wrote to DEC proposing a narrower project: weir or appurtenant-structure removal with directly related stream restoration. DEC confirmed that the Hackensack River Resilient NY report could serve as the qualifying report, with Pascack Brook material as a supplement.

The deadline question

Engineers raised legitimate concerns about completing more technical work by June 5, but those concerns had become tied to an assumption that a new qualifying study was mandatory. I circulated DEC’s written guidance and clarified that a limited support memo could address competitiveness. The grant consultant then confirmed that June 5 was not a required comparable-study deadline for the chosen pathway.

The second turning point: permission

Eligibility didn’t give the Village permission to build on County-controlled land. I worked on the site-control questions and pushed for documentation of RCDA support. The team obtained an RCDA letter for the application, while the grant consultant noted that easement and long-term site-control requirements could remain. I also specified six technical inputs for the engineering handoff: estimates, schedule, permits, mapping, maintenance, and construction assumptions.

What changed, and what did not

The effort moved from scattered studies and competing interpretations toward a defined project and application package with State eligibility guidance, engineering inputs, and County support documentation. I can’t claim a grant award, completed construction, or reduced flooding from this record. What it changed for me was how I understand research: sometimes the discovery isn’t a new answer, but why existing answers haven’t produced action.

The story in four frames

30-second read
01The problem

The price was not the cost

The Village charged for permits and applications, but older flat amounts didn’t measure the staff, inspection, legal, and consultant effort behind them. A zoning text amendment, for example, carried a $375 base fee.

Judgment callDon’t assume a published price measures the cost of delivering the service.

I followed the work behind each fee

I spoke with departments about application steps, repeat visits, inspections, professional review, and overhead. A small residential application and a large development place very different burdens on the Village, yet legacy categories blurred them.

I structured tiers by application complexity, separated Village fees from replenishable applicant-funded review escrow, and added categories for repeat processing. Revenue wasn’t the only constraint: fees also had to be intelligible, lawful, and workable for staff, and escrow can’t be counted as revenue.

I reviewed the prior schedule, gathered operational context across departments, proposed tiers by application size and complexity, distinguished municipal fees from replenishable consultant escrows, and modeled revenue scenarios to support Board decisions.

A fee schedule is also a service map: it shows the work that happens after an application is submitted.

Yesterday’s prices, today’s work

The Village was operating under financial pressure while some service fees still reflected an older way of working. A zoning text amendment carried a $375 base fee in the older schedule, but processing modern applications could involve staff reviews, inspections, consultants, public hearings, and follow-up. I was asked to review the fee schedule, and at first it sounded like a pricing exercise. I couldn’t justify the prices without understanding the work.

I went to the people doing the work

I spoke with staff across departments and examined the existing code. I asked what happened after applications arrived, when repeat inspections were needed, how many staff hours went into processing, and which tasks drew in outside planners, engineers, or legal counsel. Not every applicant consumed the same resources, yet the schedule didn’t consistently distinguish those demands.

The reframe

I stopped treating the schedule as a list of amounts to increase and started treating it as a description of municipal work. The revised model distinguished Village revenue fees from replenishable applicant-funded professional-review escrows, added more specific tiers based on the application’s characteristics, and addressed repeated processing or public-resource use where appropriate. A large multifamily project shouldn’t automatically be priced like a small homeowner’s request.

The politics of adoption

The recommendation still had to survive the real decision process. I wanted to recover more costs, but the Board had to weigh community effects, legal authority, and political acceptability. Not all amounts were adopted at the levels I recommended. The revised fee schedule nevertheless went to a vote and was adopted.

What the numbers actually mean

I prepared models showing the possible revenue effect under explicit activity assumptions: one broader scenario estimated $98,250 in additional annual fees, and a narrower land-use scenario estimated $40,000. They overlap, are not additive, and are not receipts.

What stayed with me

The most important deliverable wasn’t a better-looking table. It was a way to connect what the government charges to what delivering the service actually requires, and a proposal that moved through the Board rather than stopping as analysis.

What changed

First-page conversion rose from 33% to 45%.

The first page of the listing flow wouldn’t let people continue without a reservation number many owners don’t have, couldn’t be saved, and restarted if you went back a step. 01 / The symptom

People left on page one.

KOALA saw 75% of users leave within the first page of the listing process, and had done little research on why.

02 / The finding

The flow demanded paperwork users didn’t have.

It wouldn’t let you proceed without a reservation number, which deeded and floating-week owners rarely use. You couldn’t save progress, going back restarted the listing, and nothing said up front what details you’d need.

03 / The response

Make progress recoverable.

Save progress, make some fields optional, and say up front what’s needed. Conversion on that page rose from 33% to 45%.

Working framework / The Translation Gap

Nine places a public service can lose someone.

The need a resident expresses and the response a public institution delivers are not always the same thing. I use these nine dimensions to ask where the translation breaks down, rather than assuming the problem is a bad form or an unhelpful employee.

This is my developing diagnostic framework, not a validated measurement instrument. It guides questions to test with staff, residents, and service data.

Resident effort

How many calls, trips, documents, and retries are needed?

Fragmentation

How many teams must coordinate for one request?

Discoverability

Can a person find the right service without knowing its department?

Resolution rate

Does a need get resolved, or only acknowledged?

Socioeconomic burden

Who pays more in time, money, and uncertainty?

Government capacity

Do staff have the authority, tools, and budget to act?

Trust

Will the person expect a credible answer and follow-through?

Political voice

Whose needs reach decision-makers, and whose do not?

Downstream cost

What does an unresolved problem cost later?

About

I’m a researcher and service designer working from inside local government. In Spring Valley I ran a one-person research and service-design practice: I investigated problems, shaped the proposed solutions, drafted the language and workflows, and pushed the work toward decisions. I brought in engineers, counsel, grant consultants, and staff when their specialized knowledge was needed, but there was no separate UX or research team behind me.

My projects cross websites, regulation, public finance, and infrastructure, not because I’m collecting disciplines but because public problems don’t respect department boundaries.

I care about the unglamorous part of good design: who owns the next step, what the rules allow, what the system can actually deliver, and how we know it worked.

I’m interested in service design, research, civic technology, and strategy roles where those skills belong together.
