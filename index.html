A resident comes with a task

A resident looking for a permit shouldn’t need to know which department handles it before they can begin. But municipal websites often reflect how government is organized internally rather than how residents experience a need. In Spring Valley, forms, payments, department pages, and service instructions lived in different places. The challenge wasn’t only visual inconsistency. A resident could find a document and still have no clear idea how to finish the task.

I followed the path beyond the link

I reviewed the website’s navigation, organized a public Forms Index, and worked on homepage hierarchy and access to common services. Facility requests became a useful test of the whole system, because the process had no online path at all. A resident needed to select a facility, choose a date, explain the event, and wait for staff approval. Behind the scenes, someone had to check availability, insurance, fees, and scheduling rules.

What I had to give up

My ideal experience was simple: pick the place, request the time, understand exactly what happens next. EvoGov’s native tools imposed real limits. The portal required login, permissions were too broad for some intended viewers, not every paper-form rule could be enforced automatically, and seasonal sunset cutoffs weren’t supported as requested. I worked within the controls we could validate instead of inventing an interface staff couldn’t operate.

The observable result

The Forms Index went live. Seven facility and stage resources were configured for public requests. Two controlled test records exercised confirmation and cancellation, and the public calendar reflected those status changes. That is a usable foundation, not a finished payment-and-permit service. The October 2026 flow map describes the next release, scheduled for early November 2026, with one reference number and approval before payment; it is not the operating system yet.

What stayed with me

The visible page was only half the problem. A simpler resident experience holds up only when staff can receive the work, interpret its status, and carry it to completion.

The crowded street was not the whole problem

Spring Valley had a transportation problem: congestion, passenger-safety concerns and disputes about unlicensed rides. But taxis were also indispensable to residents and a livelihood for people living paycheck to paycheck. Some had already invested in licenses and compliance. The easy answer—fewer cabs and harder penalties—risked squeezing those people while leaving larger coordinated operations intact. I couldn’t solve one problem by creating another.

The person driving was not always the person controlling

I recognized a parallel to New York City’s struggle to regulate Uber and Lyft: the person behind the wheel is visible, but dispatch, routing, payment and control can live elsewhere. This was my connection, not a solution handed to me. After talking with smaller operators and reading Chapter 239, I realized we did not necessarily need a wholly new enforcement apparatus. We needed to make the existing definitions account for the actual business arrangement.

The definition became a design decision

The critical design question became what makes someone an operator. I drafted definitions based on dispatching, routing, fee collection, shared infrastructure and real control, rather than relying only on a vehicle’s appearance or a contractor label. The proposed framework tied operator, vehicle, driver and trip together so the party organizing the service could not vanish behind the individual driver.

Fairness changed the draft

This could not be a law written only for enforcement. Smaller drivers were supporting families; a registration fee or extra requirement could have real consequences. I explored proportionate fees, protections for legitimate owner-operators and checks against bigger networks fragmenting themselves into paper entities. I also had to think about privacy, due process and the cost of recordkeeping. Regulation had to see the pyramid above the driver without crushing the people at its base.

Where I draw the outcome line

The Village Board adopted the resulting vehicle-for-hire local laws. This account explains my research and the definition-level changes I developed; it does not claim that every draft line is the final codified text, or that congestion and illegal operation have already fallen.

What stayed with me

I learned that a fair rule has to find the party with the power to change the behavior—not automatically punish the person easiest to stop.

The problem was not new

Every time Pascack Brook flooded around Memorial Park, Spring Valley faced the consequences of a long-running problem. The park is one of the Village’s central recreational spaces, and nearby properties and infrastructure were also at risk. The Village was in deficit. Commissioning a fresh qualifying study would have made a funding application harder to pursue. I thought the hardest part might be discovering the engineering answer. I was wrong about where the real difficulty sat.

Following the paper trail

I looked for previous flood studies and found that the County and other agencies had already commissioned relevant work. Some hazard-mitigation sections were redacted or difficult to access. I submitted County FOIL requests for engineering records, consultant work, and the basis and status of a possible historical Village contribution. I wanted to know what had already been studied, who controlled the affected corridor, and what commitments were documented before anybody spent money or assigned blame.

Why the politics mattered

The Village was seeking County cooperation while also trying to untangle old financial obligations from earlier administrations. The work touched County-controlled stream areas and rail infrastructure. I couldn’t approach the problem as though the Village owned everything, and I couldn’t afford to damage working relationships by making claims the documents didn’t yet establish. The difference between acknowledging responsibility and granting a practical approval became essential.

The first turning point: study eligibility

The broad engineering solution wasn’t automatically suitable for the Resilient Watersheds Grant. DEC flagged gaps in the site-specific study’s future-flow analysis, and some interventions were outside the grant’s eligible scope. I wrote to DEC proposing a narrower project: weir or appurtenant-structure removal with directly related stream restoration. DEC confirmed that the Hackensack River Resilient NY report could serve as the qualifying report, with Pascack Brook material as a supplement.

The deadline question

Engineers raised legitimate concerns about completing more technical work by June 5, but those concerns had become tied to an assumption that a new qualifying study was mandatory. I circulated DEC’s written guidance and clarified that a limited support memo could address competitiveness. The grant consultant then confirmed that June 5 was not a required comparable-study deadline for the chosen pathway.

The second turning point: permission

Eligibility didn’t give the Village permission to build on County-controlled land. I worked on the site-control questions and pushed for documentation of RCDA support. The team obtained an RCDA letter for the application, while the grant consultant noted that easement and long-term site-control requirements could remain. I also specified six technical inputs for the engineering handoff: estimates, schedule, permits, mapping, maintenance, and construction assumptions.

What changed, and what did not

The essential change was the ability to seek grant funding without buying a new qualifying study. DEC confirmed that an existing report could be used for the narrowed scope, and we obtained the County support needed at application stage. We had a viable way to apply despite the deficit. That was not a grant award or a finished flood fix—but it removed a costly obstacle that had kept the project stuck.

Yesterday’s prices, today’s work

The Village was operating under financial pressure while some service fees still reflected an older way of working. A zoning text amendment carried a $375 base fee in the older schedule, but processing modern applications could involve staff reviews, inspections, consultants, public hearings, and follow-up. I was asked to review the fee schedule, and at first it sounded like a pricing exercise. I couldn’t justify the prices without understanding the work.

I went to the people doing the work

I spoke with staff across departments and examined the existing code. I asked what happened after applications arrived, when repeat inspections were needed, how many staff hours went into processing, and which tasks drew in outside planners, engineers, or legal counsel. Not every applicant consumed the same resources, yet the schedule didn’t consistently distinguish those demands.

The reframe

I stopped treating the schedule as a list of amounts to increase and started treating it as a description of municipal work. The revised model distinguished Village revenue fees from replenishable applicant-funded professional-review escrows, added more specific tiers based on the application’s characteristics, and addressed repeated processing or public-resource use where appropriate. A large multifamily project shouldn’t automatically be priced like a small homeowner’s request.

The politics of adoption

The recommendation still had to survive the real decision process. I wanted to recover more costs, but the Board had to weigh community effects, legal authority, and political acceptability. The Village Board adopted my proposed fee schedule. It moved from analysis into the Village’s operating framework.

What the numbers actually mean

I prepared models showing the possible revenue effect under explicit activity assumptions: one broader scenario estimated $98,250 in additional annual fees, and a narrower land-use scenario estimated $40,000. They overlap, are not additive, and are not receipts.

What stayed with me

The most important deliverable wasn’t a better-looking table. It was a way to connect what the government charges to what delivering the service actually requires, and a proposal that moved through the Board rather than stopping as analysis.

03 / About

How I got here

My story ↓What I believe ↓Where I thrive ↓My working framework ↓

My career path has been anything but straight. I’ve designed interior plantscapes for corporate clients, grown medical cannabis for a dispensary, worked in hospitality, and organized flowers for weddings. The titles have nothing in common, but the work does.

In corporate interior design, clients often knew a design felt wrong but couldn’t say why, so I learned to read their reactions and work out what they meant. In hospitality, I learned how to talk to people who were hungry, tired, and out of patience, and still leave them feeling taken care of. In every job I had to understand the limits (light, budget, time, regulation), be honest about what was possible, and make the experience feel a little more human than it had to be.

Now I do that for the Village of Spring Valley, where I went to high school and once lived. Civil service wasn’t the plan, but I wanted to help a community I have a stake in. Many residents here work multiple jobs and don’t have time to sit through meetings or find out what help exists, so I try to make the village easier to use and easier to trust.

My approach

Listen first, including to what people can’t quite say. Name the constraints early, and don’t promise what I can’t deliver. The small details are what people actually run into, so I sweat them.

Beyond work

NYT puzzles, wrestling my oversized dog, anything on wheels or a board, and a good trail.

What I believe

Design and research matter to me because I’ve seen how the same system can create opportunity for someandobstacles for others.

It works. Just not for everyone.

Two things can be true at once. A system can work exactly as intended and still leave people behind. That is the gap I work in: between what a system was built to do and who it actually serves.

The cost doesn’t disappear. It lands on a coworker running on fumes or a resident who can’t get a straight answer, and it lands hardest on the people with the least time, money, and cushion.

For me, research means listening to people’s stories and understanding the weight they carry. Data can tell you where a process breaks. Only people can tell you what it costs them. That’s why I’m building a way to measure what gets lost in translation.

So I ask who built it, for whom, what has changed since, and who has the power to change it. Great design can’t save bad policy, and great policy falls apart without careful follow-through.

Working framework / The Translation Gap

Nine places a public service can lose someone.

The need a resident expresses and the response a public institution delivers are not always the same thing. I use these nine dimensions to ask where the translation breaks down, rather than assuming the problem is a bad form or an unhelpful employee.

This is my developing diagnostic framework, not a validated measurement instrument. It guides questions to test with staff, residents, and service data.

Resident effort

How many calls, trips, documents, and retries are needed?

Fragmentation

How many teams must coordinate for one request?

Discoverability

Can a person find the right service without knowing its department?

Resolution rate

Does a need get resolved, or only acknowledged?

Socioeconomic burden

Who pays more in time, money, and uncertainty?

Government capacity

Do staff have the authority, tools, and budget to act?

Trust

Will the person expect a credible answer and follow-through?

Political voice

Whose needs reach decision-makers, and whose do not?

Downstream cost

What does an unresolved problem cost later?
