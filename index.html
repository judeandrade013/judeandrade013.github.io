Design Researcher · Behavioral Insights & Service Innovation

I’m Jude Andrade. I study how people make their way through products and public services, and what gets in their way.

For the past year I’ve been the only researcher in the Mayor’s Office of the Village of Spring Valley, New York, a municipality of about 33,000 people. The work has ranged from facility booking and problem reporting to taxi regulation, flood funding, and the village fee schedule. From 2020 to 2022 I was the first dedicated UX researcher at KOALA, a timeshare marketplace, where I set up the company’s research practice. I’ve also done independent research for travel and hospitality clients, including Lonely Planet.

I studied psychology, and I came to research by way of plant design, cannabis cultivation, hospitality, and wedding florals. Many of my projects started with a reasonable assumption that turned out to be incomplete, such as the idea that unlicensed taxis were mainly an enforcement problem, or that a flood grant would require paying for a new engineering study. Finding out what’s actually going on is the part of the job I like best.

Selected work, 2020–2026

  • 01Taxi and for-hire transportation

    Spring Valley’s taxi law could see the driver, but not the business behind the ride

    Complaints about unlicensed cabs pointed toward tougher enforcement. Talking with every local cab company and reading the village code suggested the law’s definitions were the weak point, and that new penalties would mostly land on small, licensed drivers.

    Led the research and drafted the definitions · Local laws adopted by the Village Board · Enforcement effects not yet measured
  • 02KOALA, product research

    Why timeshare owners gave up on the first page of a listing

    The listing flow asked for a reservation number many owners never receive, and it lost their progress if they went back a step. Interviews with owners showed where the flow broke down. Letting owners save progress and making some fields optional raised the success rate on that page.

    First dedicated UX researcher, 2020–2022 · First-page success rate 33% → 46%
  • 03Municipal website and facility requests

    What happens after a resident submits a form?

    Booking a village park or community center used to take two to four trips to Village Hall. I mapped the request from both the resident’s side and the Clerk’s, then set up and tested online requests for seven facilities within the limits of the village’s CMS.

    Led the research and the build · Forms Index and online requests live · Online payment awaiting approval
  • 04Memorial Park flooding

    The park floods every few years. What would it take to qualify for state funding?

    A new engineering study looked like a prerequisite for a state grant, at a time when the Village was running a deficit. Records requests and written guidance from the state DEC showed that an existing regional report could qualify a narrower project.

    Led the investigation · Grant eligibility confirmed · No award or construction yet
  • 05Master fee schedule

    What does it actually cost the Village to process a land-use application?

    Fees didn’t track the work: departments estimated a minor site plan at about three days of staff time and a major one at more than a week. I inventoried all 172 fees, traced the roughly 45 that have an application, proposed tiers by size and complexity, and separated outside review costs into applicant escrow.

    Led the analysis and proposal · Adopted by the Village Board · Revenue figures are projections

How I got here

My story ↓What I believe ↓Where I thrive ↓My working framework ↓

My career path has been anything but straight. I’ve designed interior plantscapes for corporate clients, grown medical cannabis for a dispensary, worked in hospitality, and organized flowers for weddings. The titles have nothing in common, but the work does.

In corporate interior design, clients often knew a design felt wrong but couldn’t say why, so I learned to read their reactions and work out what they meant. In hospitality, I learned how to talk to people who were hungry, tired, and out of patience, and still leave them feeling taken care of. In every job I had to understand the limits (light, budget, time, regulation), be honest about what was possible, and make the experience feel a little more human than it had to be.

Now I do that for the Village of Spring Valley, where I went to high school and once lived. Civil service wasn’t the plan, but I wanted to help a community I have a stake in. Many residents here work multiple jobs and don’t have time to sit through meetings or find out what help exists, so I try to make the village easier to use and easier to trust.

My approach

Listen first, including to what people can’t quite say. Name the constraints early, and don’t promise what I can’t deliver. The small details are what people actually run into, so I sweat them.

Beyond work

NYT puzzles, wrestling my oversized dog, anything on wheels or a board, and a good trail.

What I believe

Design and research matter to me because I’ve seen how the same system can create opportunity for someandobstacles for others.

It works. Just not for everyone.

Two things can be true at once. A system can work exactly as intended and still leave people behind. That is the gap I work in: between what a system was built to do and who it actually serves.

The cost doesn’t disappear. It lands on a coworker running on fumes or a resident who can’t get a straight answer, and it lands hardest on the people with the least time, money, and cushion.

For me, research means listening to people’s stories and understanding the weight they carry. Data can tell you where a process breaks. Only people can tell you what it costs them. That’s why I’m building a way to measure what gets lost in translation.

So I ask who built it, for whom, what has changed since, and who has the power to change it. Great design can’t save bad policy, and great policy falls apart without careful follow-through.

Working framework / The Translation Gap

Nine places a public service can lose someone.

The need a resident expresses and the response a public institution delivers are not always the same thing. I use these nine dimensions to ask where the translation breaks down, rather than assuming the problem is a bad form or an unhelpful employee.

This is my developing diagnostic framework, not a validated measurement instrument. It guides questions to test with staff, residents, and service data.

Resident effort

How many calls, trips, documents, and retries are needed?

Fragmentation

How many teams must coordinate for one request?

Discoverability

Can a person find the right service without knowing its department?

Resolution rate

Does a need get resolved, or only acknowledged?

Socioeconomic burden

Who pays more in time, money, and uncertainty?

Government capacity

Do staff have the authority, tools, and budget to act?

Trust

Will the person expect a credible answer and follow-through?

Political voice

Whose needs reach decision-makers, and whose do not?

Downstream cost

What does an unresolved problem cost later?
