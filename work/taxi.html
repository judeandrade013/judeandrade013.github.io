01 / Taxi and for-hire transportation

↑ All work

The taxi law could see the driver, but not the business behind the ride

In 2026, Spring Valley received more than 80 complaints about unlicensed “pirate” cabs in a single month. The obvious response was stricter enforcement. I spent the project trying to understand who actually organized the rides, and I ended up recommending changes to how the law defines an operator instead.

My role
Led the research and drafted the definitional changes. Counsel, enforcement stakeholders, and operators gave input; the Village Board approved them.
Methods
Conversations with every local cab company, licensing-record review with the Village Clerk, comparison of Chapter 239 with NYC and similar municipalities
Outcome
The Village Board adopted the resulting vehicle-for-hire local laws (2026)
Not yet known
Effects on enforcement and congestion

A crowded curb and an obvious answer

The complaints described congestion, passenger-safety concerns, and disputes over unlicensed rides. They were real. But the licensed industry was large: the Village had issued about 800 licenses to drive the year before, and for many drivers this was their main income. Some had paid for licensing and compliance specifically so they could work legally.

Capping the number of cabs and raising penalties would have been visible, and it would have looked like action. The trouble was that the people easiest to fine were the drivers standing at the curb, and many of them were already following the rules. A tougher regime would add cost for them while the operations coordinating unlicensed rides stayed out of view.

Who controls a ride?

I met with each local cab company and went through the licensing records with the Clerk. I also read Chapter 239, the village’s vehicle-for-hire law, alongside New York City’s rules for dispatch bases and the codes of towns comparable to Spring Valley.

The New York City comparison turned out to be the useful one. The city’s long fight over regulating Uber and Lyft came down to a distinction between the person driving and the system that assigns, routes, and charges for the trip. When I reread Chapter 239 with that distinction in mind, the weak point was in its definitions: if a dispatcher or fleet owner organized the rides, the law gave enforcement little to hold onto beyond the driver.

That changed the question I was working on. Instead of asking how to catch more unlicensed drivers, I started asking what should make someone count as an operator.

Defining an operator by what it does

I drafted definitions based on what a business actually does: dispatching and routing rides, collecting fees, sharing infrastructure such as a payment system, and controlling which vehicles go out. How a vehicle looks, or whether a driver is labeled a contractor, would no longer settle the question. The drafts tied operator, vehicle, driver, and trip together, so the business organizing a ride couldn’t disappear behind the person driving it. They also added checks against a larger operation splitting itself into several companies on paper.

Fairness changed the draft

A law written only for enforcement would have hurt the drivers it was supposed to protect. Many smaller drivers were supporting families, and a new registration fee or recordkeeping requirement could cost them real money. I tested the drafts against smaller operators’ circumstances, and four choices shaped the result.

The choiceWhere I landed
Cap the number of vehicles, or hold networks accountable?I explored capacity controls, but a cap couldn’t substitute for identifying who operates a ride.
Penalize drivers, or make operators responsible?Requirements that reach the businesses controlling dispatch and vehicle access.
Uniform fees, or tiered?Fees that scale with the size of the operation, to protect smaller compliant businesses.
Judge affiliation by branding, or by evidence of control?By conduct and coordination. Some drafts explicitly avoid presumptions based on how vehicles look.

I also had to weigh privacy, due process, and the cost of keeping trip records, since every new requirement lands on small operators too.

What was adopted, and what isn’t known yet

The Village Board adopted the resulting vehicle-for-hire local laws. This account describes my research and the definition-level changes I developed. It doesn’t claim that every line I drafted made it into the final codified text, and it doesn’t claim that congestion or illegal operation has fallen. Neither has been measured yet.

What I took from it

The most useful thing I did was question a legal category before proposing a new penalty. The complaints were about drivers, but some of the rides were organized further up the chain, and a fair rule needed to reach the people making those decisions.
