01 / Taxi and for-hire transportation

↑ All work

The taxi law could see the driver, but not the business behind the ride

Policy design · Regulatory research · Stakeholder research · Operational accountability

In 2026, Spring Valley received more than 80 complaints about unlicensed “pirate” cabs in a single month. The obvious response was stricter enforcement. I spent the project trying to understand who actually organized the rides, and I ended up proposing changes to how the law defines and regulates an operator.

My role
Led research with local transportation operators, examined licensing practices and comparable regulations, and developed proposed changes to Chapter 239, including new regulatory classifications, operator responsibilities, and enforcement provisions
Methods
Conversations with local cab companies, licensing-record review with the Village Clerk, comparison with NYC and other municipalities
Outcome
The Village Board adopted vehicle-for-hire legislative reforms in 2026
Not yet known
Effects on unlicensed operations, compliance, and congestion

Starting from enforcement

Spring Valley was receiving complaints about unlicensed taxis, congestion, and passenger safety. At first, I approached the problem as an enforcement issue: how could the Village better identify and penalize people operating illegally?

But conversations with local taxi companies complicated that assumption.

Licensed drivers were already paying to operate within the Village; it had issued about 800 licenses to drive the year before. Some were independent operators supporting their families. Others worked through larger dispatch or fleet arrangements. Stronger penalties alone could increase the burden on compliant drivers without addressing the businesses coordinating transportation behind the scenes.

The more I examined the industry, the clearer it became that the law wasn’t adequately distinguishing between the person driving a vehicle and the business controlling its operation.

Understanding who actually controlled a trip

I met with local taxi companies, reviewed licensing records with the Village Clerk, and compared Chapter 239 with regulatory approaches in New York City and other municipalities. New York City’s long fight over regulating Uber and Lyft was the most useful comparison, because it turned on the same distinction between the person driving and the system that assigns, routes, and charges for the trip.

I looked beyond vehicle ownership to understand the operational relationships between drivers, dispatchers, fleet owners, and passengers.

Who assigned rides? Who controlled access to vehicles? Who collected payments? What happened when a driver stopped working with a company? Could an operator avoid licensing obligations simply by organizing trips through another entity?

These questions shifted my focus from individual violations to the structure of the transportation system itself.

Introducing Transportation Network Providers into the local framework

One of my contributions was introducing the Transportation Network Provider (TNP) classification into the proposed Village code.

The existing regulatory categories centered on drivers, vehicles, and owners. I proposed a definition that also captured entities arranging or coordinating trips, whether they operated through a call center, radio dispatch, software platform, or another system.

The important distinction was functional: an organization could be responsible for transportation operations even if it didn’t directly own the vehicle or employ the driver.

I also drafted provisions requiring applicable Village licenses for TNPs and multi-vehicle operators coordinating trips within the Village, regardless of where the business was physically located. That addressed a gap that driver-focused enforcement couldn’t close on its own.

Designing accountability beyond licensing

Defining the operator was only one part of the problem.

I also developed proposed requirements for disaffiliation and debranding: what should happen when a driver or vehicle was no longer connected to a licensed transportation company?

The draft established responsibilities for notifying the Village, deactivating dispatch access, removing identifying markings, and providing proof that a vehicle was no longer operating under a company’s authority. I developed accompanying enforcement provisions addressing licensing violations and vehicle impoundment.

Together, these proposals treated accountability as something an operator keeps up over time, from the license application through the day a driver or vehicle leaves.

Balancing enforcement with the realities of small businesses

I had to weigh public safety and enforceability against the cost of compliance.

A small owner-operator and a company coordinating multiple vehicles don’t necessarily place the same demands on the Village. Treating them identically could create disproportionate burdens without making enforcement more effective.

I explored ways to distinguish operator categories, assign responsibilities according to operational control, and prevent larger entities from avoiding requirements through fragmented ownership or dispatch arrangements. I also had to weigh privacy, due process, and the cost of keeping trip records, since every new requirement lands on small operators too.

The choiceWhere I landed
Cap the number of vehicles, or hold networks accountable?I explored capacity controls, but a cap couldn’t substitute for identifying who operates a ride.
Penalize drivers, or make operators responsible?Requirements that reach the businesses controlling dispatch and vehicle access.
Uniform fees, or tiered?Fees that scale with the size of the operation, to protect smaller compliant businesses.

The aim was a framework that matched how transportation in the Village actually worked.

What was adopted, and what isn’t known yet

The Village Board adopted vehicle-for-hire legislative reforms in 2026. My work contributed research, regulatory definitions, proposed accountability mechanisms, and enforcement language for that legislative process.

The immediate outcome was a more developed policy framework for distinguishing drivers from businesses that coordinate and control trips.

What remains unmeasured is whether the reforms reduce unlicensed operations, improve compliance, or decrease congestion. The draft provisions described here shouldn’t all be assumed to appear unchanged in the final adopted law.

What I learned

I began by asking how to enforce the existing rules more effectively. I ended up questioning whether those rules described the industry accurately enough to enforce in the first place.

That changed the work. Instead of treating the visible driver as the entire problem, I looked at the relationships and decisions behind the service, and used that understanding to develop a more accountable regulatory structure.
