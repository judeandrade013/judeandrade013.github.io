05 / Master fee schedule

What does it actually cost the Village to process an application?

I was asked to review Spring Valley’s fee schedule while the Village’s finances were under pressure. I ended up mapping the work behind 172 fees and rethinking how applications are classified and charged. The Village Board adopted the revised schedule.

My role
Led the fee schedule review, researched departmental processes, analyzed the existing code, developed revised fee categories and pricing structures, and prepared supporting financial and legislative materials

01 Context

What the old fees didn’t tell me

I was asked to review the Village’s fee schedule while its finances were under pressure. Some charges were low for the amount of work behind them. A zoning text amendment, for instance, carried a $375 base fee, even though processing one could involve several departments, public hearings, outside professional review, and follow-up.

I couldn’t tell whether a fee was reasonable just by looking at the price. First I needed to know what the Village actually had to do to provide each service.

02 Approach

Mapping the work behind 172 fees

I inventoried all 172 items on the schedule and found that about 45 were tied to an application. Then I talked with three departments about how those applications moved through the Village: who received them, how staff assessed them, what needed extra review, and where delays or repeat work tended to happen. I also went back through Chapter 118 and the earlier fee structure to find categories that no longer matched how the work was done.

The differences were large. Staff estimated a minor site plan at roughly three days of work and a major one at more than a week, yet the schedule didn’t consistently distinguish between applications that far apart. At that point the job was less about updating prices and more about rethinking how services were classified and charged in the first place.

03 Decisions

Charging for the work involved

The revised schedule sorts applications by size, complexity, and the review they need. Site plans now have four tiers: minor, standard, major, and large or complex. Other categories are based on dwelling units, gross floor area, or the extent of the work. I also covered work the old schedule tended to miss, such as resubmissions, reinspections, extended processing, and some uses of public resources.

I wanted staff to be able to apply the schedule the same way every time, and applicants to know roughly what they would pay before they started.

Running the numbers

I compared the old and proposed charges and modeled the revenue effect under different assumptions about application volume. Take a 28-unit multifamily development that needs a special permit, a zoning text amendment, and standard site-plan review. Under the earlier fees I compared, those charges came to about $5,150. Under the revised schedule they come to $12,400, which is $7,250 more, not counting escrow or other charges.

Over a full year, a scenario with six multifamily special permits and ten smaller applications produced an illustrative increase of about $43,750 in those application fees. I also modeled a broader scope: about $98,250 a year across every fee on the schedule. The two cover different ground and overlap, so they can’t be added together, and both rely on activity numbers and averages that haven’t been checked against actual collections. I built them so the tradeoffs would be visible. Neither is money the Village has received.

Keeping fees and escrow apart

One of the most important parts of the redesign was separating application fees from escrow. An application fee pays for the Village’s own administrative work. Escrow is money an applicant deposits to cover outside professional services on their project, such as engineering, planning, environmental review, or legal consultation. I set up escrow tiers by project complexity, with provisions for topping up the balance when more review is needed.

The distinction matters for the math, too. A $20,000 escrow balance isn’t $20,000 of Village revenue, and counting it that way would have made the projections look much better than they were. Deposits are a third thing again: they cover a specific cost and come back if they aren’t used. An early draft of my 28-unit example had counted the $500 posting deposit as a fee; the version here doesn’t.

04 Outcome

Getting it adopted

The schedule also had to work across several departments and make sense to Village leadership. That meant writing objective categories, spelling out when outside review costs applied, and balancing cost recovery against predictability and the effect on applicants. I prepared the revised schedule and the supporting materials for legislative consideration, and the Village Board adopted it.

What was adopted, and what is still a projection

The adopted schedule is the concrete result: clearer service categories, more detailed application tiers, and a firm line between Village fees and outside review costs. The financial effect is still prospective. Measuring actual revenue, cost recovery, or any change in processing time will take collection and workload data over time.

What I took from it

I started with the question of how much the Village should charge. The more useful question turned out to be what the Village was being asked to do, and once I understood that, the pricing decisions had something solid to rest on.
