What does it actually cost the Village to process an application?

Service operations research · Financial modeling · Policy design · Organizational change

I was asked to review Spring Valley’s fee schedule while the Village’s finances were under pressure. I ended up mapping the work behind 172 fees, redesigning how applications are classified and charged, and modeling what the changes would mean financially. The Village Board adopted the revised schedule.

My role
Led the fee schedule review, researched departmental processes, analyzed the existing code, developed revised fee categories and pricing structures, and prepared supporting financial and legislative materials
Worked with
Three Village departments (how the work is done); leadership and the Village Board (the decision)

An outdated schedule was hiding the work behind public services

I was asked to review the Village’s fee schedule at a time when municipal finances were under pressure.

Some charges had remained relatively low despite the amount of administrative work involved. A zoning text amendment, for example, carried a $375 base fee under the previous structure, even though processing an application could involve multiple departments, public hearings, professional review, and follow-up.

I couldn’t determine whether a fee was reasonable from its price alone. First, I needed to understand what the Village actually had to do to provide the service.

Mapping the work behind 172 fees

I inventoried 172 fee items and identified approximately 45 tied to an application process.

I spoke with three departments to understand how different requests moved through the Village: who received them, how staff assessed them, what required additional review, and where delays or repeat work occurred.

I also examined Chapter 118 and the earlier fee structure to identify where categories had become inconsistent with current administrative practices.

The differences were significant. Staff estimated that a minor site plan could require roughly three days of work, while a major application could require more than a week. Yet the existing fee structure did not consistently distinguish between applications of substantially different complexity.

That finding changed the assignment from updating prices to redesigning how services were classified and charged.

Designing fees around the work required

I developed a revised schedule that differentiated applications by factors such as development size, complexity, and review requirements.

For site plans, I proposed four tiers: minor, standard, major, and large or complex. Other categories used dwelling units, gross floor area, or the extent of work as a basis for determining applicable charges.

I also addressed administrative work that the existing schedule did not always capture clearly, including resubmissions, reinspections, extended processing, and certain uses of public resources.

The aim was a fee structure staff could apply consistently and applicants could understand before entering the process.

Separating Village revenue from professional-review escrow

One important part of the redesign was distinguishing between application fees and escrow deposits.

A Village application fee supports the municipality’s own administrative work. Escrow funds are held to pay for outside professional services associated with an application, such as engineering, planning, environmental review, and legal consultation.

I developed replenishable escrow tiers based on project complexity, with provisions for restoring balances when additional review was required.

This distinction also mattered for financial modeling. A $20,000 escrow deposit doesn’t mean the Village has earned $20,000 in revenue. Keeping these amounts separate made the proposal easier to evaluate and kept projected receipts from being overstated.

Testing the financial implications

I compared the previous fee structure with proposed charges and modeled the potential revenue effect under different application-volume assumptions.

For example, consider a 28-unit multifamily development requiring a special permit, a zoning text amendment, and standard site-plan review. Under the selected earlier fees, those charges total approximately $5,150. Under the revised schedule, the comparable charges total $12,400. That is $7,250 in additional application fees for this illustrative project, excluding escrow and other applicable charges.

An annual scenario using six multifamily special permits and ten smaller applications produces an illustrative increase of approximately $43,750 in selected application revenue. It assumes application volumes and smaller-project averages that haven’t been checked against actual collections.

I also modeled the schedule at two broader scopes. Across all fees on the schedule, one scenario estimated about $98,250 in additional annual revenue. Limited to land-use fees, the estimate was about $40,000. These three scenarios measure different scopes and overlap, so they shouldn’t be added together, and all of them rest on limited activity data.

The point of the modeling was to make the financial tradeoffs visible without claiming revenue the Village hadn’t received.

Moving from research to legislative adoption

The proposal required more than calculating new prices.

I needed to translate the analysis into a schedule that could be administered across departments and considered by Village leadership. That meant creating objective categories, documenting when additional review costs applied, and balancing municipal cost recovery with predictability and the effect on applicants.

I prepared the revised schedule and supporting materials for legislative consideration. The Village Board subsequently adopted the fee reform.

What was adopted, and what is still a projection

The project produced a revised municipal fee framework that differentiated service types, established more detailed application tiers, and clarified the treatment of outside professional-review costs. The adopted schedule is the direct organizational outcome.

The financial effect remains prospective. Actual revenue increases, cost recovery, and changes in processing efficiency will need collection and workload data to measure.

What I learned

The original question was how much the Village should charge. The more useful question was what the Village was being asked to do.

Once I understood the work, it became possible to design a more consistent structure for classifying services, explaining costs, and making informed financial decisions.
