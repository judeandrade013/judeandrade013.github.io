05 / Master fee schedule

↑ All work

What does it actually cost the Village to process a land-use application?

Under the old schedule, Spring Valley charged a $375 base fee for a zoning text amendment, although processing one could involve staff reviews, outside consultants, public hearings, and follow-up. I was asked to review the fee schedule. I ended up mapping the work behind it, and the Village Board adopted the revised schedule I proposed.

A pricing exercise that needed more information

At first the assignment sounded like a pricing exercise. The Village was under financial pressure, and some fees reflected an older way of working. But I couldn’t justify a new number without knowing what the work behind it involved, and the fee schedule didn’t say.

Asking the departments

I inventoried all 172 items on the fee schedule. Not every fee has an application attached; for the roughly 45 that do, I traced the process from submission through review and follow-up. I asked three departments what each kind of application required and how long it took: what happened once it arrived, when repeat inspections were needed, how many staff hours went in, and which tasks pulled in outside planners, engineers, or lawyers. I also went back through Chapter 118, the existing fee code.

The work varied much more than the fees did. Departments estimated a minor site plan at about three days of staff time and a major one at a week or more. A large multifamily project shouldn’t automatically be priced like a small homeowner’s request, but the old schedule didn’t consistently tell them apart.

Two kinds of money

The most useful step was separating two kinds of money. A Village fee pays for the Village’s own work, so it can scale with the size and complexity of an application. An applicant escrow is a deposit, topped up as needed, that covers outside professional review such as consulting engineers or planners. It passes through the Village and isn’t revenue. Keeping the two apart let me set fees that reflect staff work without counting consultant costs as income.

Getting it through the Board

I proposed tiers only where the work genuinely differs. Site plans, for example, now have four levels, from minor to large or complex. Where it applied, I also accounted for repeated processing and the use of public resources. I would have liked to recover more of the Village’s costs, but the Board had to weigh the effect on the community, its legal authority, and what was politically acceptable, and the schedule had to be something staff and applicants could follow. The Village Board adopted the fee schedule I proposed.

What the numbers mean

I modeled the possible revenue effect under stated assumptions about application volume. A broader scenario estimated $98,250 in additional annual fees, and a narrower land-use scenario estimated $40,000. The two overlap, so they shouldn’t be added together, and both rest on limited activity data. They are projections. Nobody has measured actual collections yet.
