About

How I got here

Design Researcher · Behavioral Insights & Service Innovation

  • judeandrade013@gmail.com
  • LinkedIn ↗
  • New York Metro

For the past year I’ve been the only researcher in the Mayor’s Office of the Village of Spring Valley, New York, a municipality of about 33,000 people. The work has ranged from facility booking and problem reporting to taxi regulation, flood funding, and the village fee schedule. From 2020 to 2022 I was the first dedicated UX researcher at KOALA, a timeshare marketplace, where I set up the company’s research practice. I’ve also done independent research for travel and hospitality clients, including Lonely Planet.

Many of my projects started with a reasonable assumption that turned out to be incomplete, such as the idea that unlicensed taxis were mainly an enforcement problem, or that a flood grant would require paying for a new engineering study. Finding out what’s actually going on is the part of the job I like best.

Before research, I designed interior plantscapes for corporate clients, grew medical cannabis for a licensed dispensary, worked in hospitality, and arranged flowers for weddings. On paper those jobs don’t have much in common. In practice, the work overlapped more than you’d expect.

In corporate interior design, clients often knew a design felt wrong but couldn’t say why, so I learned to read their reactions and work out what they meant. In hospitality, I learned how to talk to people who were hungry, tired, and out of patience, and still leave them feeling looked after. Every job had limits, whether light, budget, time, or regulation, and I had to be honest with people about what was possible within them.

Now I do that for the Village of Spring Valley, where I went to high school and once lived. Civil service wasn’t the plan, but I wanted to help a community I have a stake in. Many residents here work more than one job and don’t have time to sit through meetings or find out what help exists, so a lot of my work is making the village easier to use.

I have a B.A. in psychology from Empire State University, an A.S. in business marketing from SUNY Rockland, and a UX design and research certificate from General Assembly.

What I care about

Two things can be true at once

I got into this work after watching the same system open doors for some people and close them for others. A process can work exactly as it was designed and still leave people behind. Most of my projects end up somewhere in that space, between what a system was built to do and who it actually serves.

When a system doesn’t work for someone, the cost doesn’t disappear. It usually lands on a staff member who is already stretched, or on a resident who can’t get a straight answer, and it lands hardest on people with the least time and money to absorb it. Data can show me where a process breaks. Talking to people is how I find out what that costs them.

So I tend to ask who built a system, who it was built for, what has changed since, and who has the power to change it now. Design can’t rescue a bad policy, and a good policy still needs someone to follow through on the details.

A framework in progress

The Translation Gap

When a resident’s request goes unresolved, it’s tempting to blame a bad form or an unhelpful employee. I’ve been developing nine questions to find where the breakdown actually happens. It isn’t a validated measurement tool. I use it to decide what to ask staff and residents, and which service data to look at.

Resident effort
How many calls, trips, documents, and retries does it take?
Fragmentation
How many teams have to coordinate for one request?
Discoverability
Can someone find the right service without knowing which department runs it?
Resolution rate
Does the need get resolved, or only acknowledged?
Socioeconomic burden
Who pays more in time, money, and uncertainty?
Government capacity
Do staff have the authority, tools, and budget to act?
Trust
Does the person expect a credible answer and follow-through?
Political voice
Whose needs reach decision-makers, and whose don’t?
Downstream cost
What does an unresolved problem cost later?

Where these come up in my work: discoverability in the website case, accountability in the taxi case, authority in the flood case, and downstream cost in the fee case.
